SR-GRPO asks whether the stable rank of a response's internal representations can be used as a reward to train the model directly. It is an unsupervised geometric reward. It computes something different from Silence the Judge: that paper takes one vector per response and compares distances between several responses, whereas here each token of a single response gets its own hidden vector, the vectors are stacked as rows of a matrix H, and stable rank = sum of all squared singular values / square of the largest singular value. If the vectors are concentrated almost entirely in one direction, the stable rank is close to 1; if they spread over many directions, it is larger. The authors give higher reward to responses with richer representational structure, but stable rank itself is no proof that an answer is correct.

During training, 8 responses are sampled from the current policy for each prompt. Scoring always uses the frozen initial reference model: the LoRA adapter is temporarily turned off, the prompt and response are fed back through the original model, the stable rank is computed from the last hidden layer, and the adapter is then turned back on for training. This way the policy cannot raise its score just by changing its own internal representations; it has to produce text that scores higher under the fixed scoring model. Each response's advantage = (stable rank − mean stable rank of the group) / (group standard deviation + ε), and training uses GRPO.

The experiments come in three groups. First: score two existing replies and see whether it picks the right one. Second: let the model answer several times and see whether this score picks out the better answer. Third: actually train the model with this score and see whether it improves. In the first group, the reply with the larger stable rank is chosen directly on RewardBench's 2,985 preference pairs. In the second, Best-of-N: 1, 4, 8 or 16 responses are generated per question and the one with the highest stable rank is picked, compared with greedy and random selection. Only in the third group is GRPO training done, tested on GPQA, MMLU-redux, MATH500, AIME, OlympiadBench, AMC and WildBench. The training baselines use the same prompts and framework but swap in an external preference reward model, Self-Reward (the model generates a 1–5 score), Perplexity (based on response probability), and IPO (scores from Yes/No probabilities). Average math accuracy goes from 28.0% to 32.4% starting from an ordinary instruction-tuned model, and from 58.5% to 64.7% starting from a reasoning-trained model. The paper also compares PCA dimension, effective rank and condition number, but mainly in the RewardBench scoring experiment; it does not run full RL with every metric. The results show that stable rank is an effective reward proxy in these settings, but they do not establish that richer representations necessarily mean more correct answers.
