Silence the Judge asks whether an LLM's internal hidden states can be used directly to score its responses, with those scores then used for reinforcement learning, so that neither reference answers nor an external LLM judge is needed. The authors observe that when a model generates several responses to the same question, the hidden vectors of correct responses tend to cluster together, while those of wrong responses are usually more scattered. Based on this, they propose Latent-GRPO. The core idea: have the model answer the same question several times, find the consensus center of these responses in representation space, and score each response by how far it is from the center. The closer to the center, the higher the reward. The whole process never needs to know which response is actually correct.

### How is the consensus center found?

Concretely, the model generates 8 full responses per question. The authors take the last-layer hidden state at the last content token of each response, so each response corresponds to one vector. Next they need a consensus center for these 8 vectors. The simplest way is to take the mean, but if a few wrong responses lie far away, they can pull the mean off. So the authors design IRCE, which finds the center by repeatedly adjusting weights. First, the 8 vectors are normalized to unit length and their mean is taken as the initial center. Then the distance from each vector to the center is computed. Vectors close to the center get larger weights and distant ones smaller weights, with weight = exp(−distance² / (2 × σ²)), where σ is the standard deviation of these distances. The center is then recomputed with these weights, and the distances and weights recomputed, for at most 5 iterations. This way, vectors that clearly stray from the other responses have less and less influence on the final center.

### How is the reward set?

Once the final center is found, the authors compute each response's reward from its distance to the center: the smaller the distance, the higher the reward. Concretely, they take the negative of the distance and rescale the scores of the 8 responses in the group to 0–1. The reward here comes entirely from hidden states; it does not check whether the final answer is correct, and no separate reward model is trained. However, the method in effect assumes that the consensus formed by most responses is more likely to point in the right direction. If the model keeps giving the same wrong answer to a question, those wrong responses may also cluster together and get high scores. This is where a geometric reward by itself cannot guarantee correctness.

### How is the model trained?

With rewards for the 8 responses, the authors train the model with GRPO. Training does not modify the hidden states directly to pull them toward the center; instead, the reward trains the model to produce responses that more easily get high geometric scores. Each time the model generates a new group of responses, the hidden states, center and rewards are recomputed.

### Experimental setup

Training uses GSM8K, MATH and Open-Platypus, with 2,000, 3,000 and 4,500 questions respectively. After training, MMLU, MATH500, BBH, AIME and other tasks test whether the model's reasoning actually improved. The baselines mainly compare different ways of building the reward while keeping the GRPO training procedure as similar as possible. Rule-based Reward checks the model's answer against the reference answer: 1 if correct, 0 if wrong. LLM-as-Judge has another LLM judge whether the response is correct, also giving 0 or 1. Latent-GRPO uses neither reference answers nor an external judge and scores by hidden-state distances between responses. This directly tests whether a reward built from the model's internal representations can match or even beat training that relies on reference answers or an external judge.

The authors also run two important ablations. The first checks which hidden states to take from a response. Besides the last token, they try averaging the hidden states of all tokens, and a weighted average of hidden states at keyword positions. This is to understand why looking only at the last token gives an effective reward. The second checks how the consensus center should be computed. They compare the plain mean, K-Means, eigenvector centrality and the proposed IRCE, to verify whether IRCE, which finds the center by down-weighting outlier vectors, is really more effective than the alternatives.

The results show that on some math and reasoning tasks, Latent-GRPO matches or beats training with rule-based rewards and LLM-judge rewards, without extra calls to a judge model. Training speed is close to rule-based verification and usually faster than an external LLM judge. However, the reported roughly 2× speedup was measured with the external judge's call rate limited, so it does not carry over directly to every training setup. In addition, for some small models, cross-task performance does not exceed the level before training. So the paper's most important contribution is not proving that "closer hidden states are necessarily more correct", but showing that one can directly compare the internal representations of several responses to the same question, build a reward from them without reference answers, and use it to train the model.
